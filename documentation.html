Legal Documentation

Laws, identity documents, and international legal framework

MYANMAR CITIZENSHIP LAWS

1948 Union Citizenship Act (Inclusive)

The original citizenship law after independence was relatively inclusive. It recognized multiple pathways to citizenship including birth, descent, and naturalization. Under this law, the Rohingya were generally recognized as citizens and participated in democratic processes, including voting and running for Parliament.

1974 Emergency Immigration Act

This act provided the legal framework for marginalizing the Rohingya. It introduced strict immigration controls and created the basis for labeling Rohingya as "illegal immigrants" despite their long-standing presence in the region.

1982 Burma Citizenship Law (Exclusionary)

The most destructive law for Rohingya rights. Key provisions:

  • Full citizenship restricted to 135 officially recognized "national races"
  • Rohingya were deliberately excluded from this list
  • Citizenship requires proof of ancestry in Myanmar before 1823
  • Created three tiers: Full Citizens, Associate Citizens, and Naturalized Citizens
  • Rohingya were rendered effectively stateless

Impact: The 1982 law made the Rohingya vulnerable to arbitrary arrest, forced labor, movement restrictions, denial of education, healthcare, and marriage rights.

IDENTITY DOCUMENTS SYSTEM

National Registration Cards (NRC)

Originally issued to Rohingya as proof of citizenship. Starting in the 1970s, authorities systematically confiscated these cards from Rohingya, replacing them with inferior documentation.

Foreign Registration Cards (FRC)

Replaced NRCs for Rohingya. These cards explicitly identified holders as non-citizens or foreigners, despite many families having lived in the region for generations.

Temporary Registration Cards ("White Cards")

Issued as a temporary measure but became permanent for many Rohingya. These cards did not confer citizenship rights and were often used to restrict movement and access to services.

National Verification Cards (NVC)

Introduced more recently as part of the "verification" process. Human rights organizations have criticized these as a tool of coercion. Many Rohingya call them the "genocide card" because accepting them is seen as admitting to being illegal immigrants.

Current Status: Most Rohingya lack any valid documentation, making them completely stateless and unable to prove their identity or claim rights.

INTERNATIONAL LEGAL RESPONSE

UN Fact-Finding Mission (2018)

The UN Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Myanmar concluded that:

  • The military committed genocide against the Rohingya
  • Crimes against humanity and war crimes were systematic
  • Recommended prosecution of senior military leaders
  • Called for an arms embargo and targeted sanctions

International Criminal Court (ICC)

The ICC has opened an investigation into crimes against humanity, including:

  • Deportation across the Bangladesh border
  • Persecution on ethnic and religious grounds
  • Other inhumane acts including sexual violence

International Court of Justice (ICJ)

The Gambia filed a case against Myanmar at the ICJ under the Genocide Convention. In January 2020, the ICJ ordered provisional measures requiring Myanmar to prevent genocide and preserve evidence.

UN General Assembly Resolutions

Multiple resolutions have called for:

  • Accountability for perpetrators
  • Safe and voluntary return of refugees
  • Restoration of citizenship rights
  • Unrestricted humanitarian access

HUMAN RIGHTS DOCUMENTATION

Key Reports & Evidence

  • UN Human Rights Council: Detailed reports on systematic violence and displacement
  • Amnesty International: Documentation of mass killings, rape, and village destruction
  • Human Rights Watch: Satellite imagery of destroyed villages
  • MSF (Doctors Without Borders): Survey of 6,700+ deaths in first month of 2017 violence
  • Fortify Rights: Evidence of military planning and preparation for attacks

Key Findings

Documentation consistently shows:

  • Systematic targeting of Rohingya civilians
  • Use of rape as a weapon of war
  • Mass burning of villages (354 documented)
  • Forced starvation and denial of humanitarian aid
  • Prevention of return and destruction of homes
